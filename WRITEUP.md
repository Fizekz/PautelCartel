# Write-up

We treated the contest as a betting problem as much as a forecasting one: a correct pick earns −log2 of its Kalshi price, so we aimed for accurate win probabilities and then picks that maximise expected points. The two of us first built forecasts separately, competing against each other, then pooled our best work: five models from one side (vote-share models with Student-t errors and calibrated logistic models of expert ratings) and a two-stage margin model with poll-error add-ons from the other, blended equally because the blend beat the five-model pool and tied the stack on the 2018 and 2022 backtests (on all races of each year, log loss 0.076 and 0.072 per race, against 0.100 and 0.095 for FiveThirtyEight's final forecasts on the same races). Inputs were MIT Election Lab returns, Cook, Sabato and Inside Elections ratings, polls from Wikipedia, VoteHub and FiveThirtyEight's archives, FEC fundraising, partisan lean, Census demographics and our own dossiers on 85 races; Kalshi's November 1 prices drove the pick rule, and our dossier writers saw them, so the dossier adjustments are partly anchored on the market. We back an underdog only where expected points favour it and our probability is at least the market's, because in backtests the long shots our models liked won far less often than predicted; that gives 17 underdog picks and 58.4 expected points under our probabilities (48.5 if the market's prices are right), against 51.3 (45.9) for always backing the favourite, and we overrode four same-party California races by hand because only one side's models covered them. The main risks: 2026's D+8.6 environment lies outside our training years, one correlated polling miss would sink most of our 15 Republican underdogs together, most House races lack polls, and our models, blend and pick rule were designed with 2018 and 2022 in view.
